01 / 12Project Overview

Project Overview

One product ecosystem. Multiple experiences. One consistent identity.

Kyvos helps enterprises bring consistent business context, performance, and governance to analytics and AI through its Semantic Layer technology.

Communicating this value to enterprise buyers is challenging. The audience includes technical architects, data leaders, analytics teams, and senior business decision-makers, each approaching the product with different expectations.

My work focused on making that experience clearer and more consistent across the places people discover, evaluate, and engage with Kyvos.

I contributed to the website experience, Semantic Layer product communication, brand guidelines, marketing campaigns, event presentations, and reusable visual systems.

Rather than treating these as isolated design deliverables, I approached them as connected parts of a larger enterprise experience.

The central design question:

How might we make a complex enterprise AI and analytics offering easier to understand, navigate, recognize, and engage with across every customer touchpoint?

02 / 12The Challenge

The Challenge

Enterprise technology is complex. Understanding its value shouldn't be.

Kyvos communicates sophisticated technology, including Universal Semantic Layer architecture, AI analytics, large-scale data performance, and enterprise governance.

Across multiple channels, that creates several design challenges.

Challenge 1 — Communicating technical complexity

Enterprise concepts such as semantic models, consistent business metrics, AI grounding, and query performance require clear explanation.

The challenge was to communicate the product's business value without sacrificing technical credibility.

Challenge 2 — Supporting website discovery

A growing collection of product pages, technical resources, case studies, reports, and campaigns creates a need for predictable navigation and content hierarchy.

Users should be able to discover relevant information and identify the next appropriate action without unnecessary effort.

Challenge 3 — Maintaining brand consistency

Website components, marketing ads, presentations, whitepapers, and event materials are created for different channels and formats.

Without shared design principles, visual experiences can become inconsistent.

Challenge 4 — Competing for attention

LinkedIn advertisements and event displays have limited opportunities to communicate value.

Information needs to be immediately understandable, readable, and relevant to the audience.

Challenge 5 — Scaling design execution

Multiple product initiatives and campaigns were running in parallel, requiring coordination between design, marketing, product, content, engineering, leadership, and production vendors.

Consistency had to be maintained without slowing down delivery.

03 / 12My Approach

My Approach

I treated brand consistency as a user experience problem.

My working approach connected five design principles:

  1. Clarity: Make complex information easier to understand.
  2. Discoverability: Help users find products, proof points, and resources.
  3. Consistency: Establish recognizable patterns across touchpoints.
  4. Reusability: Design components and rules that can be applied repeatedly.
  5. Execution quality: Ensure the final implementation preserves design intent.

The objective was not simply to make Kyvos look more polished.

It was to make the experience more coherent for users, easier to maintain for teams, and better aligned with enterprise business communication.

04 / 12Website UX & Engagement

Website UX & Engagement

From individual pages to a more connected discovery experience.

The Kyvos website serves audiences at different stages of evaluation.

Some visitors are learning about the Semantic Layer. Others are comparing platforms, reviewing technical architecture, exploring customer outcomes, or considering a demo.

My website-related assignments included:

  • Homepage and hero-section improvements
  • Navigation and responsive mega-menu designs
  • Universal Semantic Layer product pages
  • Kyvos Dialogs landing pages
  • Live Demo page design
  • Comparison page experiences
  • Customer Stories and Blog components
  • Resource page layouts
  • CTA variations and reusable promotional components
  • Benchmark and product storytelling sections
  • Desktop and mobile UI refinements

Design Decision 01 — Navigation should reflect user intent.

Problem

As product capabilities and resource content expand, navigation must support multiple user journeys without forcing everyone through the same path.

My approach

I worked on the navigation and mega-menu experience with attention to category structure, visual hierarchy, content grouping, readability, and responsive behavior.

The goal was to help visitors distinguish between product capabilities, use cases, learning resources, and conversion opportunities.

UX rationale

Recognition is generally easier than recall. Clearly grouped information reduces the need for visitors to remember where content might be located.

Intended experience

Users can more quickly recognize relevant destinations and follow a logical exploration path.

Design Decision 02 — CTAs need a clear purpose and hierarchy.

Calls to action serve different stages of the customer journey.

A visitor exploring the technology may need documentation or a Buyer’s Guide, while someone actively evaluating Kyvos may be ready for a product demo.

I worked on reusable CTA and promotional patterns with attention to:

  • Primary versus secondary visual hierarchy
  • Contrast and visibility
  • Placement within page content
  • Consistent interaction patterns
  • Alignment with surrounding messaging

The design principle was simple:

Make the next action clear without making every action compete for attention.

Design Decision 03 — Product pages should communicate value progressively.

Technical product pages need to serve both scanning and detailed reading.

For Universal Semantic Layer, Dialogs, and related pages, my work emphasized information hierarchy, visual storytelling, architecture graphics, and structured content presentation.

The intended experience was to move from a clear value proposition to product capabilities, supporting explanations, proof points, and conversion opportunities.

This established a more coherent communication flow without requiring every visitor to engage with the same level of technical detail.

05 / 12Simplifying Semantic Layer Communication

Simplifying Semantic Layer Communication

Translating technical architecture into understandable business value.

One of the most significant parts of my Kyvos work was visual communication around the Universal Semantic Layer and its relationship with enterprise analytics and AI.

The challenge was that a Semantic Layer is an architectural concept, not a conventional physical product.

It needed to be understood by both technical and nontechnical audiences.

My design approach

I worked across product pages, technical visuals, architecture illustrations, reports, campaign creatives, and event communications.

The design approach emphasized a hierarchy of information:

Business outcome → Product capability → Technical explanation → Supporting evidence

For example, instead of relying on a dense architecture illustration as the first communication layer, a design could begin with a clear outcome and progressively explain the underlying technology.

Key visual communication areas

Universal Semantic Layer

Explaining how shared business definitions and semantic models connect analytics tools, data platforms, and AI consumers.

Kyvos Dialogs

Communicating conversational and agentic analytics through approachable product storytelling, with emphasis on natural-language interaction and enterprise data insights.

AI accuracy and consistency

Supporting stories about the importance of governed business context and reliable analytics.

Performance and scalability

Designing benchmark and report visuals to help audiences scan technical comparisons and identify significant findings.

UX principle

Progressive disclosure

Introduce the information users need first, then reveal deeper technical detail as their interest and evaluation needs develop.

This helped guide the visual structure of complex enterprise content across multiple formats.

06 / 12Brand Guidelines & Design Consistency

Brand Guidelines & Design Consistency

A brand is more than its logo. It is a repeatable experience.

Kyvos appears across websites, sales presentations, LinkedIn campaigns, technical reports, webinars, conference displays, and print materials.

Each channel has different design requirements, but the brand should remain recognizable.

My brand-system work involved developing and documenting design principles across:

Visual foundation

Color usage, typography hierarchy, spacing, composition, logo treatment, and contrast.

Graphic language

Technical architecture illustrations, iconography, visual metaphors, image treatment, and layout consistency.

Digital design

Website components, cards, CTA styles, responsive patterns, content hierarchy, and accessibility considerations.

Marketing applications

Social creatives, report covers, whitepapers, campaign banners, presentations, and supporting assets.

Physical experience

Event backdrops, exhibition booths, tabletop collateral, and large-format typography.

My key design decision

Rather than defining only how the brand should look, I focused on how design rules could support repeated decisions.

For example, consistent typography establishes information priority. Reusable components support faster production. Defined layout rules help protect visual coherence across different screen and print sizes.

Design principle:

Consistency should reduce decision-making effort for both the people using the experience and the teams producing it.

07 / 12Marketing Campaign Experience

Marketing Campaign Experience

Designing for attention, comprehension, and action.

LinkedIn creates a very different interaction environment from an enterprise website.

Users are scrolling, attention is fragmented, and a campaign often has only a brief opportunity to communicate value.

My work included Kyvos campaigns and assets relating to:

  • The Semantic Layer Buyer’s Guide
  • Universal Semantic Layer
  • Enterprise AI and Semantic Layer messaging
  • AI analytics accuracy
  • Technical and educational whitepapers
  • Event promotion
  • Product-focused campaign variations

My approach to campaign design

1. Establish an immediate focal point

The headline or central visual should communicate the subject before secondary content competes for attention.

2. Prioritize mobile readability

Text size, line breaks, contrast, and image composition need to remain effective at smaller display sizes.

3. Connect the visual to the value proposition

Technical illustrations should clarify the message, not exist as decoration.

4. Preserve brand recognition

Campaigns should remain visibly connected to Kyvos through consistent typography, color, and design language.

5. Maintain an unmistakable conversion path

The creative and CTA should direct interested users toward the next relevant action.

A documented delivery outcome

For the Semantic Layer Buyer’s Guide campaign, I developed multiple creative directions and copy-based variations.

The campaign went through stakeholder review, and the October 2026 submission received approval on the first round, with positive feedback.

This demonstrated effective alignment with the approved content, brand expectations, and campaign requirements.

It is evidence of design execution efficiency, rather than proof of higher CTR or conversions.

08 / 12Enterprise Event Experience

Enterprise Event Experience

Designing beyond the screen.

A conference booth is a different type of user interface.

Visitors approach from varying distances, move through the environment, and have only a short opportunity to understand the message.

My event-related design work included Gartner Data & Analytics, Global Fintech Fest, ASUG-related assets, and other enterprise event materials.

Deliverables included exhibition backdrops, branded display surfaces, presentations, handouts, print assets, and event communications.

My design approach

Distance-based information hierarchy

The primary message needs to be recognizable from farther away. Secondary details can be positioned for closer reading.

Visual hierarchy at physical scale

Large-format design needs to consider typography size, contrast, viewing angles, monitor placement, and visual obstruction.

Real-world production constraints

I worked through artwork adjustments, print specifications, proportions, content placement, and vendor coordination to support production-ready designs.

Consistent product storytelling

The booth, supporting collateral, and event campaign materials needed to communicate a connected Kyvos identity.

The broader UX lesson

Experience design does not stop at the browser.

Physical environments also require decisions about attention, wayfinding, readability, cognitive effort, and user behavior.

11 / 12What I Learned

What I Learned

Design consistency is a business capability.

Working across Kyvos showed me that enterprise design operates as an interconnected system.

A product message developed for the website should remain understandable in a campaign.

A brand guideline should influence actual component decisions.

An event experience should reinforce the same product story that users encounter online.

And a technically accurate message is only effective when its intended audience can understand it.

The project strengthened my approach in three areas:

Systems over isolated screens

Designing repeatable patterns is more valuable than repeatedly solving the same problem in different formats.

Clarity over decoration

Enterprise audiences need useful, credible information. Visual polish supports that purpose, but cannot replace it.

Leadership through alignment

Good design outcomes depend on coordinating business goals, user needs, technical accuracy, and execution quality.
